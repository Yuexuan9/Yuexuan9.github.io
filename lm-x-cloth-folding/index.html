Engineering case study / LM-X

Cloth folding
with LM-X.

I worked on policy training, robot deployment, and evaluation for cloth folding on Astribot S1, alongside iterations of a Value-based progress evaluator. The project involved both model behavior analysis and investigation of the robot execution pipeline. Under a defined T-shirt protocol, the final LM-X evaluation completed 16 of 20 attempts.

02 / Iterations & reasoning

Turning failed rollouts into engineering decisions

In early difficult-folding tests, a policy could smooth the garment yet regress during the actual fold. I used checkpoint comparisons, progress signals, and robot motion to identify priorities for the next training and deployment iteration.

Checkpoint comparison & failure analysis

On-robot observations

I tested 0718-STEP-1, 0718-STEP-34W, 0810-STEP-1, and 0810-STEP-75000 on difficult cloth folding, reviewing grasping, smoothing, and folding as separate task stages.

0810-STEP-1 — Could smooth the garment, but showed clear regressions and incorrect actions once folding began.

0810-STEP-75000 — No successful completion was observed in this test round; inaccurate grasp locations and repeated regressions were the main failure modes.

These difficult development tests are recorded separately from the final published evaluation below; they are not a controlled 0% → 80% comparison.

Keyframe: a hypothesis for controlled ablation+

Repeated failures suggested that Keyframe conditioning might interfere with the action policy. A matched on/off ablation, holding data, initialization, and test conditions fixed, is needed to measure its effect on completion and regressions.

The engineering term Keyframe should not be equated with a single image or pose: the paper’s ETG represents an action sequence to the next semantic event. The published cloth-folding setup disables event conditioning, which alone does not establish the cause of early failures. Paper Appendix D.1 ↗

03 / Execution pipeline

Stabilizing the robot execution pipeline

I investigated motion at three levels: the predicted trajectory, action-chunk handoffs, and controller filtering. Trajectory smoothing visibly helped; chunk and SDK settings remained candidates for further comparison.

01 / OBSERVED IMPROVEMENT

Trajectory smoothing

Spikes in predicted action sequences could produce large robot motions. Gaussian and spline smoothing, with 3× interpolation to increase execution steps, visibly reduced large jitter; slight high-frequency motion remained at the end effector.

02 / TUNING STRATEGY

Temporal ensembling across chunks

Weighted averaging of overlapping predictions can reduce noise at chunk boundaries. Candidate comparisons included replanning stride 20 → 10, aggregation coefficient 0.01 → 0.005, and interpolation factor 3 → 4 to increase overlap and distribute weights more evenly.

03 / FOLLOW-UP VALIDATION

Low-level controller filtering

For residual high-frequency jitter, inspect SDK filter strength alongside tracking latency. Candidate settings reduce the primary filter scale from 0.4 to 0.2 or 0.15, and, if needed, increase trajectory Gaussian sigma from 1.8 to 2.5–3. Their effects still require matched validation.
